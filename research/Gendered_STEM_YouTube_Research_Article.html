Abstract

The release of gendered-representation debates in STEM communication motivates close empirical study of how audiences respond to female- versus male-hosted science and technology content. This study presents a web mining analysis of 34,473 top-level public comments harvested via the YouTube Data API v3 from twelve established STEM channels — six hosted by women, six by men — spanning mathematics, physics, data science, programming, and hardware. Sentiment was scored using the VADER lexicon-based model; emotion was classified with a fine-tuned DistilRoBERTa model; and toxicity, insult, and identity-attack sub-scores were produced with the Detoxify unbiased model. Two keyword lexicons flagged appearance-focused remarks and credibility-challenging remarks. Results show 63.8% positive, 21.0% neutral, and 15.1% negative sentiment overall. Female-hosted channels received a higher rate of appearance-focused remarks (3.69% vs. 3.12%) and a lower toxic-comment rate (1.95% vs. 2.85%) than male-hosted channels, while male-hosted channels attracted more credibility-challenging remarks (4.32% vs. 3.25%). Cluster-robust Generalized Estimating Equations and a mixed-effects logistic model — accounting for the nesting of comments within videos and channels — found none of these differences statistically significant (all p > .09), with a small channel-level intraclass correlation (ICC = 0.011). The appearance-remark gap was the largest and most theoretically expected effect (Cliff's δ = 0.61, p = .093) and persisted in a niche-controlled subgroup restricted to programming channels.

Index Terms — gender bias, online harassment, sentiment analysis, STEM communication, toxicity detection, YouTube comments, generalized estimating equations, mixed-effects models.

I.

Introduction

Video-sharing platforms such as YouTube have become a primary venue for informal science communication, with independent creators building large audiences around programming tutorials, physics explainers, and data-science content. As with other social media spaces, the comment sections beneath these videos are an important but under-examined site of audience–creator interaction, capable of shaping both the experience of the creator and the tone of public discourse about STEM topics. A recurring concern in media and communication research is that women who communicate science online face a qualitatively different reception than their male counterparts — one more likely to include remarks about physical appearance, challenges to their expertise, and hostile or dismissive language.

This project investigates that concern empirically using a purpose-built dataset of audience comments collected from twelve established STEM YouTube channels, evenly split between female and male hosts and spanning several sub-disciplines. Rather than relying on manual reading of a small number of threads, the study applies automated natural-language-processing tools — sentiment scoring, emotion classification, and machine-learned toxicity detection — at scale, supplemented by keyword lexicons targeting two theoretically motivated categories of gendered discourse: appearance-focused remarks and credibility-challenging remarks. Because comments are naturally nested within videos and channels, the analysis also applies clustering-aware statistical methods that prior single-metric or purely descriptive treatments of this topic often omit.

II.

Research Objectives & Questions

The overarching aim is to determine whether, and to what extent, the gender of a STEM YouTube creator is associated with measurable differences in the volume, sentiment, emotional character, and toxicity of the comments their content receives. This aim is broken into five objectives:

  1. O1 — Quantify and compare comment-level sentiment, emotion distribution, and machine-scored toxicity between female- and male-hosted STEM channels.
  2. O2 — Measure the prevalence of appearance-focused and credibility-challenging remarks directed at creators of each gender.
  3. O3 — Test whether any gender association is statistically robust once video/channel clustering and channel popularity are accounted for.
  4. O4 — Characterize channel- and niche-level heterogeneity as a potential confound alongside creator gender.
  5. O5 — Assess the reliability of automated measures through a stratified manual-coding validation sample.

Research questions

  • RQ1 — Do comments on female-hosted STEM channels differ in sentiment, emotion, or toxicity from male-hosted channels?
  • RQ2 — Are appearance-based remarks and credibility attacks differentially directed by creator gender?
  • RQ3 — Do gender differences persist once clustering and popularity are statistically controlled?
  • RQ4 — What role might content niche play as a confound?
III.

Related Work

A growing body of research documents gender asymmetries in the reception of women who communicate science and technology online. Content-analytic studies of YouTube science channels report that female hosts receive a disproportionate share of remarks about physical appearance and personal life relative to male hosts, alongside more hostile or dismissive commentary about their expertise [1], [2]. Related work using large annotated comment corpora finds that channels associated with female creators receive more sexist and hateful comments than comparable channels associated with male creators, even after accounting for content category [5]. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies of STEM YouTube specifically describe negative comments on female-hosted channels clustering around personal criticism and objectification, while comments on male-hosted channels remain concentrated on the technical content itself [9].

Methodologically, the study draws on three established NLP tools. VADER is a lexicon- and rule-based sentiment model developed and validated specifically for short, informal social-media text [3]. Detoxify is an open-source transformer-based classifier trained on the Jigsaw Toxic Comment datasets, providing a general toxicity probability alongside sub-scores such as insult, obscenity, and identity attack [4]. Emotion labels are produced with a DistilRoBERTa model fine-tuned for seven-way emotion classification [8]. For the statistical treatment of nested comments, the study follows the Generalized Estimating Equations framework of Liang & Zeger [6], which yields population-averaged, cluster-robust inference, and the random-intercept mixed-effects framework of Laird & Ware [7]. Effect sizes for the small-sample channel-level comparisons use Cliff's delta, a non-parametric measure well suited to small, non-normal samples [10].

IV.

Methodology

A. Data collection

Twelve established STEM YouTube channels were purposively sampled for a balanced comparison by creator gender across sub-disciplines: six hosted by women (Tiff In Tech — programming; Anastasi In Tech — hardware; Physics Girl — physics; Up and Atom — physics/mathematics; Python Simplified — programming; Sabine Hossenfelder — physics) and six hosted by men (3Blue1Brown — mathematics; Tech With Tim, The Coding Train, Corey Schafer, Traversy Media — programming; StatQuest with Josh Starmer — data science). For each channel, the twenty most recent eligible videos were selected (240 videos total) and all top-level public comments were downloaded via the YouTube Data API v3. Collection yielded 36,518 raw comments; after cleaning and a language-identification filter, the final analytic corpus totalled 34,473 comments (23,541 on female-hosted channels; 10,932 on male-hosted channels).

B. Measures

  • Sentiment — VADER compound score (−1 to +1) plus a positive/neutral/negative label.
  • Emotion — seven-class distribution (anger, disgust, fear, joy, neutral, sadness, surprise) via fine-tuned DistilRoBERTa.
  • Toxicity — Detoxify's continuous toxicity probability and sub-scores (insult, obscenity, identity attack, threat, sexually explicit, severe toxicity), plus a binary is_toxic flag.
  • Appearance remarks & credibility attacks — binary indicators from two keyword lexicons, audited against a manually-coded sample to characterize false-positive patterns.

C. Statistical analysis

Channel-level comparisons (n = 6 per gender) used two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests with Cliff's delta as effect size. Comment-level models accounted for the nested structure of the data: a random-intercept mixed-effects model of logit-transformed toxicity (random intercepts for channel and video) estimated the intraclass correlation attributable to channel; Generalized Estimating Equations with a logistic link, exchangeable working correlation, and channel-clustered robust standard errors were fitted for each binary outcome; and a niche-controlled subgroup comparison restricted to the six programming channels tested whether patterns held within a single content niche. All comment-level models included log-transformed view count as a covariate.

Comments skewed positive overall. Female-hosted channels averaged a slightly lower compound sentiment score than male-hosted channels (0.329 vs. 0.350), a gap that was not statistically significant at the channel level (Mann-Whitney U = 13.0, p = .485, Cliff's δ = −0.28).

B. Toxicity

Male-hosted channels showed a higher mean toxicity score (3.20% vs. 2.51%) and toxic-comment rate (2.85% vs. 1.95%) than female-hosted channels — the reverse of the direction hypothesized by prior appearance/credibility-focused literature — though not statistically significant (U = 14.0, p = .589, δ = −0.22). Channel identity, not gender, is the dominant source of variation: the highest-toxicity channel (Traversy Media, male) and the second-highest (Sabine Hossenfelder, female) sit at opposite ends of the gender split.

C. Appearance remarks & credibility attacks

Appearance-focused remarks were more common on female-hosted channels (3.69%) than male-hosted channels (3.12%) — the largest relative gap of any metric examined, and the closest to conventional significance at the channel level (U = 29.0, p = .093, Cliff's δ = +0.61 — a moderate-to-large effect indicating five of six female-hosted channels ranked above the male-hosted median). Credibility-challenging remarks showed the opposite pattern, occurring more often on male-hosted channels (4.32% vs. 3.25%; U = 13.0, p = .485, δ = −0.28).

D. Emotional tone

The discrete emotion distribution was similar across gender groups, with neutral (≈52–54%) and joy (≈23%) the dominant categories in both. The two categories with the largest relative gender gap were disgust (female 5.05% vs. male 4.06%) and fear (female 4.86% vs. male 3.97%), both modestly higher on female-hosted channels, while anger was marginally higher on male-hosted channels.

E. Clustering-adjusted models

Because channel-level tests use only twelve independent observations, comment-level models with cluster-robust inference were fitted to use the full data while correctly accounting for non-independence of comments within the same channel and video. A random-intercept mixed model of logit-transformed toxicity found no significant fixed effect of gender (β = 0.111, SE = 0.119, p = .351) or log-views (β = 0.008, p = .776). The estimated intraclass correlation attributable to channel was small (ICC = 0.011) — most variation occurs within, rather than between, channels.

F. Niche-controlled subgroup

Restricting the comparison to the six programming-focused channels (two female-hosted, four male-hosted) tested whether niche, rather than gender, drives the observed patterns. Female hosts still received a higher appearance-remark rate (3.52% vs. 2.00%) and a lower toxic-comment rate (2.46% vs. 3.62%) than male hosts — directionally consistent with the full-sample results, though the very small subgroup size precludes formal significance testing.

The appearance-remark gap was the largest and most theoretically expected effect observed — yet even it fell just short of the conventional significance threshold.

G. Manual validation

A stratified blinded sample of 200 comments was drawn for manual review against a private coding key. A parallel lexicon audit of appearance-remark matches revealed a non-trivial false-positive rate — for example, a keyword match on "hot" sometimes flagged a physics question about temperature rather than a genuine appearance remark. Keyword-lexicon indicators should therefore be read as an upper-bound proxy rather than a precise prevalence estimate.

VI.

Discussion

Across every outcome examined, point estimates pointed in a direction broadly consistent with the hypothesis that female STEM creators face more appearance-focused scrutiny, while male creators face somewhat more generic toxicity and credibility challenges. None of these differences were statistically significant once comment clustering was properly modeled — a result more muted than some prior studies reporting large, significant gender gaps in appearance-related commentary. Two explanations stand out. First, twelve channels gives limited statistical power for channel-level tests to detect anything but large effects; the appearance-remark gap (δ = 0.61, p = .093) is plausibly a real but under-powered signal. Second, channel-level heterogeneity within each gender group was substantial — a single channel's community norms and moderation practices appear to matter more for toxicity than creator gender alone, illustrated by the low ICC(channel) of 0.011.

The niche-controlled subgroup analysis offers some reassurance that the appearance-remark and toxicity patterns are not purely artifacts of the gender–niche confound in this sample, since the same directional pattern persisted among programming channels alone. Together, these results suggest gender-based audience bias in this domain, if present, operates as a modest, content- and community-dependent tendency rather than a uniform, large-magnitude effect — a picture only visible because of the cluster-aware modeling strategy used here.

VII.

Limitations

  • —Sample size: twelve purposively selected channels cannot represent the full diversity of STEM YouTube.
  • —Binary gender coding: creator gender was coded as binary from public information, erasing non-binary and gender-diverse creators and commenters.
  • —Comment visibility: only public, non-deleted, non-hidden top-level comments were collected; the most toxic content may be disproportionately excluded by moderation.
  • —Automated measurement error: VADER, the emotion classifier, and Detoxify are general-purpose, not fine-tuned to STEM-YouTube language or sarcasm; the lexicons demonstrably generate false positives.
  • —Association, not causation: the observational design supports statements of association, not causal claims.
  • —Confounded niche: gender and content niche are imperfectly separable in this twelve-channel sample.
VIII.

Conclusion & Future Work

This study applied a multi-method NLP pipeline and cluster-aware statistical modeling to 34,473 comments across twelve STEM YouTube channels to test for gender-associated differences in audience sentiment, emotion, toxicity, and content. Descriptive patterns pointed toward higher appearance-focused commentary and lower general toxicity on female-hosted channels, and higher credibility-challenging commentary and toxicity on male-hosted channels — but none of these differences were statistically robust once channel- and video-level clustering was modeled, and channel-level heterogeneity exceeded the average gender gap on most metrics. The appearance-remark disparity was the strongest signal (δ = 0.61, p = .093) and persisted directionally in a niche-controlled subgroup, making it the most promising candidate for confirmation in a larger sample.

Future work should expand the channel sample to increase statistical power, incorporate non-English and reply comments, replace keyword lexicons with fine-tuned classifiers validated against manual coding, and explore intersectional dimensions that a binary gender frame necessarily omits.
